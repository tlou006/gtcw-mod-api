# Commanders

## 1. What the five commander stats do

Every commander has five stats, shown on a 0–100 scale: **Leadership**, **Initiative**, **Administration**, **Cunning** and **Fame**. Here is what each one actually does.

> **The stat number on the portrait is a ceiling, not what you get.** A commander with no experience works at only **half** his listed stats, and reaches the full value only at full experience. A wounded or captured commander is halved again. So an 80-leadership general with little experience can perform worse than a 60-leadership veteran.

---
&nbsp;

### Leadership: keeps your men in the fight

**Best for:** brigade commanders, and any corps you expect to be pushed back.

* **Raises fighting spirit**, the hard cap on how high a unit's morale can go in battle. It is measured against your own army's average, so a leader only helps if he is better than his peers. Leadership matters most for brigade commanders: there it counts **4×** as much as fame, while for army commanders it counts only a quarter as much.
* **Shrinks every morale loss.** Morale losses in battle are multiplied by **3 to 5**, depending on how experienced the unit is and how good its commander's leadership is. A 100-leadership general takes a full point off that multiplier, for example from ×4 down to ×3, which means **a quarter less morale lost**.
* **Lifts the morale of nearby units.** Having a commander close by boosts morale, and leadership counts for about **1.7×** as much as fame here. If he is killed, leadership counts **2.5×** as much as fame in the morale shock that follows.
* **Lets units take heavier casualties** before their losses count as "unbearable".
* **Softens a unit's first battle.** A unit that has never fought is capped at 60% morale; a top leader buys back up to **20 points** of that.
* **Cuts losses on a retreat.** A retreating unit loses extra men. Under a 90-leadership general it loses about **6% extra**; under a 30-leadership one, about **42% extra**. No other stat does this.
* **Naval firepower.** An admiral's leadership raises his fleet's firepower against both ships and forts.

---
&nbsp;
### Initiative: reach, tempo and aggression

**Best for:** your commander-in-chief, and formations that need to move and cover ground.

* **Widens command range and combat range** of every formation on the campaign map. Command range decides how much of your army is within a commander's influence at all, so this matters a lot.
* **Slows the readiness drain from marching.** At 100 initiative, readiness wears down at only about **a third** of the normal rate. But no formation can do better here than your **commander-in-chief's** initiative allows, so a cautious C-in-C holds back your whole army.
* **Makes AI generals bolder.** A cautious AI general needs about **80%** of your strength before it attacks. A 100-initiative one will attack at **less than half** (47%), and will launch an all-out assault at **1.76 to 1** odds where a cautious one waits for **3 to 1**. Bold AI generals also launch offensives on the campaign map more readily.
* **At sea:** fleets start engagements from further away and try to ram more often.
* **Causes feuds.** A subordinate whose initiative is very different from his superior's, **higher or lower**, will start feuding. A 40-point gap gives about a **10% chance per hour**, so a feud within a day is all but certain. While feuding, the formation is taken out of your hands, its orders take **3×** as long, and it may charge the nearest enemy on its own. **Political** generals are twice as likely to charge off as West Pointers, and four times as likely as volunteers. (See Section 4.)

---
&nbsp;
### Administration: logistics and fleets

**Best for:** large armies, and admirals.

* **Faster readiness recovery**, up to **a third faster** at 100.
* **Better supply lines during a battle.** This does not affect supply on the campaign map. As an example, a brigade at the bottom of the chain might get **72%** supply under a 50-administration commander but only **62%** under a 0-administration one.
* **Fewer overnight desertions.** Administration and fame count equally here.
* **Less fatigue** when men and horses are in poor condition.
* **Fleets move, repair and resupply faster.**
* **Larger fleets.** An admiral at 100 administration can command **twice** as many ships as one at 0. Unusually, experience does not count here: a green admiral can command as many ships as a veteran with the same rating.

---
&nbsp;
### Cunning: scouting, raiding and cavalry

**Best for:** cavalry, and any detached command.

* **Intelligence gathering.** Cunning has the strongest effect here of any stat on anything in the game.
* **Concealment.** Your formation is harder for the enemy to spot.
* **Raiding and guarding** are more effective.
* **Sieges** are more effective.
* **Fewer losses when skirmishing**, up to **25% fewer** at 100 (in auto-resolved battles).
* **Cavalry take fewer casualties** under a cunning commander (in auto-resolved battles). It is the only stat that directly saves men in combat.
* **Garrisons slip away** instead of being captured.
* **Cunning AI generals burn their depots** rather than let you capture them.

---
&nbsp;
### Fame: reputation as a stat

**Best for:** army commanders, and political generals.

* **Raises fighting spirit**, just like leadership, but fame matters most at the **top** of the chain: for army commanders it counts **4×** as much as leadership.
* **Fewer overnight desertions**, equal to administration.
* **A small morale boost to nearby units**, worth less than half of what leadership gives.
* **Generates state support, but only for political generals.** A political general produces state support while he holds a command, and fame multiplies it by up to **3.5×**.
* **Decides promotions and succession.** Fame feeds the ranking that decides who gets promoted, who replaces a fallen commander, and who becomes commander-in-chief.
* **Causes feuds.** A subordinate who is **more famous** than his superior is more likely to feud. A less famous one is less likely to.
* **Famous and defamed status.** After each battle, the best performer can become **famous** (fame counts **×1.4**) and the worst can become **defamed** (fame counts **×0.7**). Defame wears off after about five months. (See Section 2.)

---
&nbsp;
### Which stats can improve?

| Stat | Can it grow? |
| --- | --- |
| **Experience** | Yes, about 20 points per year of service, up to your nation's military experience level |
| **Fame** | Yes, through battles, promotions and events. Drops 5–30% each time he comes back from a wound |
| **Initiative** | Only down: a commander returning from a wound loses 5–30% of it |
| **Leadership** | No |
| **Administration** | No |
| **Cunning** | No |

> **The one exception: military schools.** Building military schools in a commander's **home state** slowly raises his leadership, administration, cunning and initiative all at once. Nothing he does in the field will ever improve them. These gains also don't show up in the stat breakdown tooltip.

## 2. Fame and defame

After every battle, the game grades every commander who took part, and **his fame goes up or down by that grade**. On top of that, the single best commander on each side may be made **famous**, and the single worst may be **defamed**.

| Status | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Famous** | His fame counts **×1.4** |
| **Defamed** | His fame counts **×0.7** |

---
&nbsp;
### How a commander is graded

* **Kill ratio:** the casualties his men inflicted, divided by the losses they took, capped at **5 to 1**. A commander whose men inflicted no casualties at all is graded as if they traded 1 for 1.
* **Plus** the experience he gained in the battle.
* **Minus** the share of his units that routed.

That grade is then scaled by his level of command. An army commander's grade counts **2.6×** as much as a brigade commander's (a division commander's 1.3×, a corps commander's 1.8×). Three things add to a good grade:

* **Printing works** in your nation boost the fame your commanders earn.
* A commander who was **wounded** in the battle gets **20% more** fame from a good result.
* The **Embedded Reporters** campaign perk (corps and above) multiplies it.

As a rough guide, a brigade commander who kills two men for every one he loses gains about **1 point** of fame. An army commander with the same ratio gains about **2.6 points**. A bad result takes fame away in the same way.

---
&nbsp;
### Becoming famous

Only the **best-graded** commander on each side can become famous, and only if his grade clears a bar. The bar depends on how the battle went **for you**, and it is the same bar for both sides' commanders.

Kill ratio needed to become famous (with no routs and no printing-works bonus):

| Battle result for you | Brigade | Division | Corps | Army |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major victory | 4.0 to 1 | 3.1 to 1 | 2.2 to 1 | 1.5 to 1 |
| Minor victory | 5.0 to 1 | 3.8 to 1 | 2.8 to 1 | 1.9 to 1 |
| Minor defeat | out of reach | 4.6 to 1 | 3.3 to 1 | 2.3 to 1 |
| Major defeat | out of reach | out of reach | 4.4 to 1 | 3.1 to 1 |

"Out of reach" means the kill ratio alone would have to beat the 5-to-1 cap. Experience gained in the battle and printing works can still push a commander over.

* **Big wins make heroes, and so do senior commands.** An army commander can become famous with a modest kill ratio. A brigade commander needs a lopsided rout of the enemy and a big victory to go with it.
* **Famous is for life.** Famous status never wears off. A commander who is already famous can't be made famous again, and if the best commander in a battle is already famous, **nobody** becomes famous from that battle.
* **Other ways to become famous:** choosing the **Blockade Busters** fleet perk makes its admiral famous on the spot, and some naval and raiding actions do too.
* **Milestone battles.** A side's first major land victory makes the winning general famous, gives him a fame bonus, and **defames the losing general**. The Confederacy's first major victory on Union soil does the same.

---
&nbsp;
### Becoming defamed

Only the **worst-graded** commander on each side can be defamed, and only if his grade is below zero. That takes **both** losing more men than he killed **and** having a large share of his units rout. A commander whose men inflicted no casualties at all is graded as an even trade, so it takes repeated routs to defame him.

How hard each one is, by the battle's result for you:

| Battle result for you | Becoming famous | Becoming defamed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Major victory | Easiest | Hardest |
| Minor victory | Easy | Hard |
| Minor defeat | Hard | Easy |
| Major defeat | Hardest | Easiest |

* **Bad appointments defame too.** Putting a general in a command whose highest allowed rank is three or more steps below his own defames him. Any command where he outranks the post costs him fame.
* **Losing a milestone battle** (see above) defames the losing general.

---
&nbsp;
### Defame wears off, and only happens once

* Defame clears itself about **five months** later, but only while he is on duty. A wounded or captured commander stays defamed until he's back.
* Becoming famous clears it immediately.
* **Once a commander's defame has cleared, he can never be defamed again** for the rest of the campaign, either by battles or by bad appointments. Only losing a milestone battle can still do it.
* A famous commander who gets defamed loses his ×1.4 and takes the ×0.7 instead. When the defame clears, he gets his ×1.4 back.

---

## 3. Wounds, capture and death

### When a commander is at risk

* **Every time his unit takes casualties, the game rolls for him.** The bigger the share of the unit lost in that hit, the bigger the risk.
* **In a firefight** he can be killed or wounded. A wound is about **five times** as likely as death, and a wound takes priority over a death, so commanders are killed even less often than that suggests.
* **In melee** there's also a chance he's **captured** if his unit routs, and capture takes priority over both.
* Commanders of **skirmishers, scouts, couriers and detachments** are never at risk.

| | Battles you fight | Auto-resolved battles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rolls per casualty | Once | **Twice**, so about double the risk |
| Superior commanders at risk | **No**, only the commander of the unit being hit | **Yes**, up to 10 levels up the chain when the enemy is close, with the risk falling sharply the higher up he is |

> **Auto-resolving is how your army commanders get killed.** In a battle you fight yourself, a general is only at risk through the unit he directly commands.

---
&nbsp;
### While he's out

* A wounded, captured or killed commander works at **half** his stats.
* He keeps his post, at half strength, until his formation is out of battle. Then he's replaced.

---
&nbsp;
### Coming back

| State | Comes back? | Typical wait | Lasting cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wounded** | Yes | About **17 months** on average | Loses **5–30%** of his fame and, separately, 5–30% of his initiative |
| **Captured** | Yes, faster the higher his rank | About **2.5 years** for the most senior generals. The most junior officers may never return during the war | None |
| **Killed** | No | — | — |

> **Every wound makes him permanently worse.** A twice-wounded general has lost a real chunk of his fame and initiative, and nothing will give it back.

---
&nbsp;
### Replacing him costs readiness

* When a commander is lost, his **best direct subordinate** is promoted into the post. That subordinate's old post is then filled by **his** best subordinate, and so on down the chain.
* **Every promotion costs every attached unit 10 points of readiness.** Lose a corps commander and you pay at the corps, the division that filled the gap, and the brigade below that. Readiness decides which orders you're allowed to give, so a decapitated corps is slow for reasons the casualty report won't mention.
* If no subordinate is suitable, the game searches the officer pool. If that fails too, it **invents a commander** with random stats: **10–70** in everything for American commanders, **50–90** for European ones.
* Basic garrisons can never get a historical officer, so every garrison is led by an invented one averaging about **40** in everything.

---

## 4. Feuds

A feud is a commander refusing to work with his superior. It only happens **during battles**, and it can happen to the enemy's commanders as well as yours.

### When feuds start

Every game hour in battle, each unit checks against its direct superior:

> **Chance of a feud per hour = initiative gap (either direction) + how much more famous the subordinate is − 30**

The figures used are the ones the commanders actually work at, so experience and famous status both count.

| Subordinate | Superior | Chance per hour | Feud lasts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initiative 50, fame 50 | Initiative 50, fame 50 | None | — |
| Initiative 90, fame 50 | Initiative 50, fame 50 | **10%** | About 30 minutes |
| Initiative 20, fame 50 | Initiative 80, fame 50 | **30%** | About 1.5 hours |
| Initiative 90, fame 90 | Initiative 50, fame 40 | **60%** | About 3 hours |

* **The initiative gap counts both ways.** An overly cautious subordinate quarrels just as readily as an overly eager one.
* **The fame gap only counts one way.** A subordinate more famous than his boss is more likely to feud, and one less famous is less likely to.
* **The bigger the mismatch, the longer the feud.**
* **These are hourly chances, so even small ones add up.** A 10% chance means a feud within the day; 60% means one within a couple of hours. Any visible gap means the feud **will** happen; the only question is when.

---
&nbsp;
### What a feud does

* **The formation leaves your control.** It is handed to a separate AI that is neither you nor the enemy.
* **It picks a random stance:** none, screen, defend or attack. It never picks an all-out assault.
* **Its orders take 3× as long.**
* **It may charge the nearest enemy on its own.** The chance grows with the **square** of his initiative, so a 90-initiative commander is over three times as likely to charge as a 50-initiative one. **Political** generals are twice as likely to charge as West Pointers, and volunteers half as likely.

---
&nbsp;
### Avoiding feuds

* **Keep initiative similar** up and down the chain of command. Don't just pick the boldest man for every post.
* **Put your most famous generals at the top.** A superior more famous than his subordinates cancels out some of the initiative gap.
* **Political generals belong under famous, bold superiors.** That shrinks both parts of the feud chance at once, and keeps the state support they generate.
